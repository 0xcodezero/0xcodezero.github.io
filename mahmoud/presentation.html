TAME: Overview & Historical Context

Definition

Transcatheter Arterial Micro-Embolization (TAME)

Minimally invasive interventional procedure targeting pathological neovascularization in chronic inflammatory musculoskeletal conditions

Historical Milestones

  • 2012: Dr. Yuji Okuno initiates TAME development
  • 2015: First publication - Knee OA (11 patients, 12-month follow-up)
  • 2018: Expanded to frozen shoulder treatment
  • 2020: Tendinopathies and plantar fasciitis applications
  • 2024-2025: International adoption, multiple RCTs, 2-year outcome data

Key Characteristics

🎯
Target-Specific

Selectively embolizes abnormal neovessels while preserving normal vasculature

💊
Disease-Modifying

Addresses root cause (inflammation/neoangiogenesis) rather than masking symptoms

⚡
Minimally Invasive

Small arterial puncture, outpatient procedure, rapid recovery (1-2 days)

🔬
Evidence-Based

70-86% success rates, sustained benefits up to 24 months

Global Impact

Adopted in Japan, Taiwan, Germany, Singapore, USA, and expanding worldwide

Pathophysiology: Neoangiogenesis & Chronic Pain

The Vicious Cycle

1

Initial Injury/Degeneration

Mechanical stress, trauma, or degenerative changes initiate tissue damage

→
2

Inflammatory Response

Release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6)

→
3

Neoangiogenesis

VEGF and Ang-1 stimulate formation of abnormal, immature vessels

→
4

Neoinnervation & Pain

Unmyelinated sensory nerve fibers accompany new vessels, creating hyperalgesia

→
5

Chronic Inflammation

Leaky vessels perpetuate inflammatory state, feeding back to step 2

Characteristics of Pathological Neovessels

  • Structural: Immature, fragile, highly permeable
  • Functional: Leaky endothelium allows inflammatory mediator extravasation
  • Neurological: Co-localization with substance P+ nerve fibers
  • Clinical: Correlate with pain severity and functional impairment

Key Research

"Neoangiogenesis is key in the progression of osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and hemophilic arthropathy. Neurogenesis can be up-regulated in parallel with angiogenesis, contributing to chronic pain development."

- Arthritis Research & Therapy, 2024

Inflammatory Cascade in Osteoarthritis

Molecular Mechanisms

Primary Insult

Mechanical stress, micro-trauma, aging

Synovial Inflammation

  • Synoviocyte activation
  • Macrophage infiltration
  • Pro-inflammatory cytokine release

Key Mediators

IL-1β TNF-α IL-6 PGE2 VEGF Ang-1

Angiogenic Response

  • Endothelial cell proliferation
  • Basement membrane degradation
  • Immature vessel formation

Pain Sensitization

  • Nerve fiber sprouting
  • Substance P release
  • Central sensitization

TAME Interrupts This Cascade

Mechanism: By occluding pathological neovessels, TAME:

  1. Reduces inflammatory mediator delivery to target tissue
  2. Decreases nerve fiber stimulation
  3. Allows tissue remodeling in less hypoxic/inflammatory environment
  4. Breaks the self-perpetuating inflammatory-angiogenic cycle

VEGF & Biomarkers in OA

Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF)

Role in OA Pathogenesis

  • Potent Angiogenic Factor: Stimulates endothelial cell proliferation and migration
  • Vascular Permeability: Increases vessel leakiness, perpetuating inflammation
  • Correlation: Synovial VEGF levels correlate with pain severity and radiographic progression
  • Therapeutic Target: Embolization reduces VEGF-driven neovascularization

Clinical Evidence: GAE patients showed significant reductions in serum VEGF and other OA-associated biomarkers at 3 and 12 months post-procedure

JVIR, 2024

Other Key Biomarkers

Angiopoietin-1 (Ang-1)

Works synergistically with VEGF to stabilize new vessels and promote vascular maturation

Matrix Metalloproteinases (MMPs)

Degrade cartilage matrix; elevated in OA. Embolization may reduce MMP activity

C-Reactive Protein (CRP)

Systemic inflammatory marker; decreases post-GAE indicating reduced inflammation

Nerve Growth Factor (NGF)

Promotes pain fiber sprouting; co-localizes with neovessels in OA synovium

Clinical Implications

Biomarker monitoring may help predict treatment response, guide patient selection, and assess disease-modifying effects of TAME

Embolic Agent: Imipenem/Cilastatin

Pharmacological Properties

Chemical Composition

  • Imipenem: β-lactam antibiotic (normally)
  • Cilastatin: Renal dehydropeptidase inhibitor
  • Combination: Poorly soluble in liquid → forms particles when suspended

Particle Characteristics

  • Size: 10-70 μm (mean 29.2±12.0 μm)
  • Shape: Predominantly polygonal (69.72%)
  • Distribution: Optimal for microvessel occlusion
  • Stability: Gradual dissolution over time

Embolic Behavior

  • Occlusion Time: Immediate upon injection
  • Dissolution: 75% size at 82±10.7 min, 50% at 89.3±9.14 min
  • Recanalization: 90 min - 48 hours in normal tissue
  • Inflamed Tissue: Prolonged occlusion in hyperemic/inflamed areas

Advantages

  • ✓ Temporary Embolization: Reduces risk of long-term ischemic damage
  • ✓ Anti-inflammatory Effects: Cilastatin may provide additional therapeutic benefit
  • ✓ Selective: Preferentially occludes slow-flow pathological vessels
  • ✓ Safe Profile: Extensive safety data from musculoskeletal applications
  • ✓ Cost-Effective: Widely available antibiotic formulation

Standard Preparation Protocol

Dosage: 0.2 GM Imipenem/Cilastatin

Dilution: 10 mL contrast medium (iso-osmolar preferred to reduce pain)

Injection: 0.2 mL aliquots followed by flush

Monitoring: Fluoroscopic assessment of "blush" disappearance

Note: Multiple injection cycles may be performed at 20-minute intervals to achieve complete embolization

Recent Evidence (2024)

"IPM/CS has been commonly used for temporary embolization in musculoskeletal conditions, tumor-related bleeding, and inflammatory hyperemia. Unlike permanent materials, its transient nature effectively manages pain and inflammation while reducing long-term ischemic complications."

- CVIR Endovascular, 2024

Embolic Agent: Microspheres (Nexsphere)

Nexsphere Characteristics

Physical Properties

  • Material: Gelatin-based bioabsorbable microspheres
  • Size Range: 100-300 microns (μm)
  • Shape: Spherical, calibrated
  • Biocompatibility: Biodegradable over weeks to months

Mechanism of Action

  • Mechanical occlusion of target microvessels
  • Induces endothelial damage and thrombosis
  • Gradually absorbed, allowing vessel remodeling
  • Longer-lasting than imipenem/cilastatin but still temporary

Preparation Protocol

1

Inject 3 mL saline into vial

2

Shake vigorously for several minutes

3

Add 3 mL contrast medium

4

Shake again to ensure uniform suspension

5

Shake before each injection to prevent settling

Clinical Application

Injection Technique

  • Volume: 0.2 mL aliquots
  • Delivery: Slow injection through microcatheter
  • Flush: After each aliquot with contrast/saline
  • Monitoring: Real-time fluoroscopy for stasis assessment

Alternating Protocol (Okuno Method)

Many operators alternate between imipenem/cilastatin and microspheres:

  1. 0.2 mL Nexsphere + flush
  2. 0.2 mL Nexsphere + flush
  3. 0.2 mL Imipenem/Cilastatin + flush
  4. 0.2 mL Imipenem/Cilastatin + flush
  5. Repeat cycle across all target vessels

Rationale: Combined approach may optimize vessel occlusion while maintaining safety

IPM/CS vs. Microspheres

Property IPM/CS Nexsphere
Particle Size 10-70 μm 100-300 μm
Occlusion Duration 90 min - 48 hrs Weeks - Months
Target Vessel Level Smaller capillaries Arterioles
Cost Lower Higher

Clinical Trials Update (2024-2025): NCT05917028 is evaluating Nexsphere-F specifically in knee OA patients, with preliminary results expected in 2025.

Shoulder TAME: Indications & Patient Selection

Primary Indication: Adhesive Capsulitis (Frozen Shoulder)

Clinical Definition

  • Progressive pain and restricted active/passive range of motion
  • Typically affects external rotation > abduction > internal rotation
  • Duration >3 months despite conservative treatment

Underlying Pathology

  • Synovial inflammation and capsular fibrosis
  • Neovascularization with accompanying nerve fiber sprouting
  • Capsular thickening and contracture

Patient Selection Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

  • ✓ Chronic shoulder pain >3-6 months
  • ✓ Failed conservative management (PT, NSAIDs, corticosteroid injections)
  • ✓ Significant functional impairment (VAS >4/10, restricted ROM)
  • ✓ MRI evidence of capsular thickening/inflammation
  • ✓ Patient desires minimally invasive alternative to surgery
  • ✓ Able to tolerate arterial catheterization

Exclusion Criteria

  • ✗ Active infection (septic arthritis)
  • ✗ Severe contrast allergy
  • ✗ Uncorrected coagulopathy
  • ✗ Pregnancy
  • ✗ Severe renal insufficiency
  • ✗ Complete rotator cuff tear requiring surgical repair
  • ✗ Glenohumeral instability

Other Shoulder Applications

Post-Herpetic Neuritis

Chest/back pain around shoulder region following herpes zoster. Target vessels supply painful dermatome.

Rotator Cuff Tendinopathy

Chronic tendinosis with failed conservative treatment. Target suprascapular and circumflex vessels.

Subacromial Bursitis

Refractory inflammation. Embolization of thoracoacromial and suprascapular branches.

Clinical Evidence (2024)

71.7% Success Rate
7.8 → 2.2 VAS Pain Reduction (6 months)
100% Technical Success
0% Major Adverse Events

Large retrospective study (128 procedures, 118 patients), 2024

Shoulder TAME: Equipment Setup

Arterial Access

Preferred Approach: Ipsilateral Transradial

Access Site:

Ipsilateral radial artery (same side as affected shoulder)

Advantages:
  • Shorter distance to target vessels
  • Patient comfort (can lie supine)
  • Immediate ambulation post-procedure
  • Lower complication rate vs. femoral
Alternative:

Femoral approach if radial access inadequate (requires longer catheter: 100-107 cm)

Sheath

  • Size: 3 French
  • Placement: Radial artery, 3 fingerbreadths above wrist crease
  • Technique: Out-of-plane ultrasound guidance

Guiding Catheter

  • Type: JR 2.5 (similar to Cobra 4F but less curved)
  • Size: 3.3 French
  • Length: 60-67 cm (ipsilateral radial)
  • Purpose: Navigates to subclavian artery, selects branch vessels

Microcatheter

  • Size: 1.6-1.9 French
  • Type: Soft, torqueable
  • Technique: Use as "wire" over 0.014" guidewire
  • Note: No need for stiff 0.035" wire

Accessories

  • Y-Connector: Prevents blood backflow
  • Three-Way Stopcock: Controls catheter hemostasis
  • Guide Wire: 0.014" soft/floppy

Contrast & Embolic Agents

  • Contrast: Iso-osmolar preferred (reduces pain)
  • IPM/CS: 0.2 GM in 10 mL contrast
  • Nexsphere: 100-300 μm prepared as described

Adjunctive

  • Nerve Block: Local anesthetic (optional suprascapular nerve block)
  • Steroid: Kenacort 0.1 mL + 3.9 mL local anesthetic for periosteal injection at tender points

Local Anesthesia Protocol

Site Preparation:

3 fingerbreadths above wrist crease

Technique:

Out-of-plane needle insertion with skin pinch

Injection:

Subcutaneous infiltration, then peri-arterial

Wait Time:

1 minute before arterial puncture

Post-Procedure:

3-minute compression, then band for 1 hour

Shoulder Vascular Anatomy

Subclavian Artery & Branches

Understanding the complex vascular supply to the glenohumeral joint is essential for successful TAME

Subclavian Artery

Thyrocervical Trunk
  • Suprascapular Artery (Most Important)
    • 5-6 anatomical variants
    • Supplies superior/posterior capsule
    • Medial blush on angiography
  • Transverse Cervical Artery
  • Ascending Cervical Artery
  • Inferior Thyroid Artery
Axillary Artery
  • Thoracoacromial Artery
    • Acromial branch
    • Supplies anterosuperior capsule
  • Subscapular Artery
    • Circumflex Scapular (inferomedial capsule)
    • Descending branch (medial scapula)
  • Anterior Circumflex Humeral
    • Small, ascending
    • Supplies humeral head, long head biceps
    • Critical for anterior pain
  • Posterior Circumflex Humeral
    • Larger vessel
    • Lower axillary level
    • Supplies posterior capsule
Coracoid Branch
  • Very small vessel under coracoid process
  • Requires injection through mother catheter

Clinical Pearls

  • Suprascapular Dominance: Most important contributor in adhesive capsulitis
  • Anastomoses: Rich collateral network - complete embolization requires addressing multiple feeders
  • Variants: Suprascapular artery has 5-6 origin variants - must identify at angiography
  • Safety: Vertebral artery proximity - rotate catheter UP and LATERAL when entering thyrocervical trunk

Six Main Target Vessels for Shoulder Embolization

1

Suprascapular Artery

Origin: Thyrocervical trunk, middle upper subclavian

Blush Location: Medial aspect of glenohumeral joint

Variants: 5-6 anatomical variations

Injection: 0.3 mL × 4 = 1.2 mL total

Adjunct: Can add suprascapular nerve block at end

Importance: ★★★★★ Most critical vessel

2

Thoracoacromial Artery

Access: Pull catheter laterally from thyrocervical with anticlockwise rotation

Target: Acromial branch

Technique: Microcatheter without wire

Can repeat: Yes, at end of procedure

3

Circumflex Scapular Artery

Location: Medial aspect of axillary artery

Branches: Circumflex (inferomedial capsule) & Descending (medial scapula)

Blush: Inferomedial glenohumeral capsule

4

Anterior Circumflex Humeral

Size: Small ascending branch

Origin: Junction of axillary/subclavian

Supply: Humeral head, long head biceps

Clinical: Important for anterior shoulder pain, frozen shoulder, biceps tendinopathy

Injection: 0.2 mL + 0.2 mL

5

Posterior Circumflex Humeral

Size: Larger than anterior

Level: Lower axillary, junction upper 1/3 & middle 1/3 scapula

Access: Look downward and laterally

6

Coracoid Branch

Size: Very small

Location: Just under coracoid process

Technique: Inject from microcatheter WITHIN mother catheter (no wedge)

Safety: Avoid deep catheter insertion

Technical Principles

✓ No need to insert microcatheter deeply

✓ No wedge embolization, especially for small feeders like coracoid

✓ Compare blush before and after at each vessel

✓ Return to vessels at end for repeat embolization if residual blush

✓ Total procedure time: approximately 1 hour
