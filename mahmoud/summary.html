Key Takeaways

TAME Overview: Trans-arterial Micro-Embolization is a groundbreaking minimally invasive technique pioneered by Dr. Yuji Okuno starting in 2012, with first publication in 2015.
Mechanism: Targets pathological neoangiogenesis (abnormal new blood vessels) that sustain chronic inflammation in musculoskeletal conditions.
Established Applications: Knee osteoarthritis, frozen shoulder, tendinopathies, plantar fasciitis with 70-86% improvement rates.
Emerging Applications: Coccydynia (tailbone pain) showing strong theoretical basis, awaiting clinical trials.
Embolic Agents: Imipenem/Cilastatin (50-150 µm particles) or Nexsphere gelatin particles (100-300 µm) for temporary vessel occlusion.
International Adoption: Spreading from Japan to Taiwan, Germany, Singapore, and other countries worldwide.
Advantages: Minimally invasive, rapid recovery (1-2 days), targets root cause, preserves anatomy, reduces medication dependence.
Paradigm Shift: TAME addresses the inflammatory source rather than merely blocking pain signals, representing a disease-modifying approach.

5. Shoulder TAME - Technical Guide
Technical

Focus: Adhesive Capsulitis (Frozen Shoulder) Treatment

Procedure Setup
  • Access: Ipsilateral transradial approach with 3 Fr sheath
  • Catheter: 3.3 Fr JR catheter (60-67 cm for ipsilateral, 80-107 cm for contralateral)
  • Technique: Microcatheter as wire with Y-connector and three-way valve
  • Imaging: Subtraction at outer 1/2 of subclavian artery
Six Main Target Vessels
  1. Suprascapular Artery (Most Important)
    • Origin: Thyrocervical trunk (from subclavian)
    • Five anatomical variants exist
    • Injection: 0.3 ml × 4 times = 1.2 ml
    • Avoid vertebral artery by rotating catheter upward/laterally
    • Can add suprascapular nerve block
  2. Thoracoacromial Artery
    • Access by pulling catheter laterally with anticlockwise rotation
  3. Circumflex Scapular Artery
    • Located at medial aspect of axillary artery
    • Circumflex branch supplies inferiomedial glenohumeral capsule
    • Descending branch along medial scapula
  4. Anterior Circumflex Humeral Artery
    • Small branch ascending from axillary/subclavian junction
    • Supplies humeral head and long head of biceps
    • Important for anterior shoulder pain and biceps tendinopathy
    • Injection: 0.2 ml + 0.2 ml
  5. Posterior Circumflex Humeral Artery
    • Larger vessel at lower axillary level
    • At junction of upper 1/3 and middle 1/3 of scapula
  6. Coracoid Branch
    • Very small vessel under coracoid process
    • Inject through microcatheter within mother catheter
    • No wedge injection due to small size
Embolic Agents

Imipenem/Cilastatin

  • Dosage: 0.2 GM diluted in 10 ml contrast
  • May use iso-osmolar contrast to reduce pain
  • Injection: 0.2 ml then flush, repeat 3-4 times

Nexsphere Gelatin Particles

  • Size: 100-300 microns
  • Preparation: Add 3 ml saline, shake for minutes, then 3 ml contrast
  • Always shake before injection
  • Alternating injection with Imipenem
  • Injection: 0.2 ml then flush
Technique Notes:
  • No need to insert microcatheter deeply
  • No wedge embolization for small feeders
  • Compare blush before and after
  • Return to vessels at end for repeat embolization if needed
Special Cases: Post-Herpetic Neuritis

For chest/back pain around shoulder region:

  • Use metallic skin markers to identify pain sites
  • Additional targets: Deep cervical, superior intercostal, descending cervical, transverse cervical arteries
  • Target muscular branches of trapezius at pain site
  • Avoid unnecessary embolization if no joint pathology

9. TAME in MSK Pain Management and Coccydynia
Most Comprehensive
MG

Dr. Mahmoud Ghalab

Lecturer & Consultant of Interventional Radiology | Fellow, Hiroshima University, Japan

Presentation Venue: SGH (likely Singapore General Hospital), July 2025

Subtitle: "Can TAME Technique Change the Game?"

TAME Overview

Full Name: Trans-arterial Micro-Embolization

Pioneer: Dr. Yuji Okuno

Timeline: Started 2012, first publication 2015

First Study: Genicular Artery Embolization (GAE) for knee OA

  • 11 patients
  • 12-month follow-up
  • Evaluated pain relief and functional improvement

Mechanism of Action

Targets and blocks abnormal micro-vessels (neoangiogenesis) that develop in areas of chronic inflammation, thereby reducing pain. These vessels are fragile, leaky, and promote sensitization.

Established Applications

  • Knee osteoarthritis
  • Frozen shoulder
  • Bursitis
  • Tendinitis and teno-synovitis
  • Coccydynia (emerging)
Coccydynia (Tailbone Pain)

Pathophysiology

  • Definition: Localized pain in coccyx region
  • Etiology: Trauma, repetitive strain, childbirth, idiopathic
  • Trigger: Sitting, leaning back, rising from seated position
  • Key Mechanism: Chronic inflammation cycle

Current Interventional Radiology Approaches

Ganglion Impar Blocks Local anesthetic + steroids to block ganglion impar nerve bundle
Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA) Heat destroys nerve tissue for longer-lasting relief
Neuromodulation Spinal cord stimulation (SCS) and peripheral nerve field stimulation (PNFS)
Pelvic Embolization Well-established for chronic pelvic pain and pelvic congestion syndrome
Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA) - Detailed Protocol

Mechanism

  • High-frequency alternating current (350-500 kHz)
  • Target temperature: 80-90°C for 60-90 seconds
  • Creates precise thermal lesion disrupting pain signals
  • Nerve regeneration occurs over 6-18 months

Target Nerves

  • Ganglion Impar (Walther's Ganglion): Retroperitoneal structure at sacrococcygeal junction providing sympathetic innervation to perineum, distal rectum, anus, and coccyx
  • Sacrococcygeal Nerves: Branches of sacral plexus (S4-S5, coccygeal nerves)

Access Routes

  • Transsacrococcygeal (through sacrococcygeal junction)
  • Paramedian (lateral to junction)
  • Anococcygeal (via anococcygeal ligament)
  • Intercoccygeal (between coccygeal segments)

Procedure Steps

  1. Patient Positioning: Prone with pillow under pelvis to reduce lumbar lordosis
  2. Imaging Setup: Fluoroscopy in AP and lateral views
  3. Local Anesthesia: 1-2% lidocaine infiltration
  4. Needle Placement: 22G RF cannula with 5-10mm active tip to target nerve
  5. Sensory/Motor Testing: Sensory at 50Hz (0.3-0.5V); Motor at 2Hz (1.5-2V)
  6. Lesion Creation: 80-90°C for 60-90 seconds per target
Technical Pearls:
  • Always perform diagnostic block before RFA
  • Use multi-planar imaging for accurate placement
  • Consider pulsed RFA for reduced neuritis risk
  • Multiple lesions may be needed for complete coverage

Patient Selection

Inclusion Criteria:

  • Chronic coccydynia (pain >3-6 months)
  • Failure of conservative management
  • Positive diagnostic nerve block (≥50% relief)
  • Localized pain without radiation
  • Pain exacerbated by sitting/direct pressure

Exclusion Criteria:

  • Active infection at procedure site
  • Uncorrected coagulopathy
  • Pregnancy
  • Unstable fracture requiring surgical fixation
  • Malignancy or infection as cause

Recovery Timeline

Phase Timeline Description
Immediate Post-Procedure 0-3 days Local soreness, possible temporary pain increase. Rest and anti-inflammatory medication recommended.
Early Recovery 3-14 days Gradual reduction in discomfort. Initial pain relief begins.
Intermediate Phase 2-6 weeks Maximum therapeutic benefit achieved. Return to normal activities.
Long-term Results 6-18 months Sustained pain relief. Possible nerve regeneration may require repeat procedure.

Complications

Common Side Effects (Mild):

  • Post-procedure soreness (1-3 days)
  • Temporary numbness in treated area
  • Bruising at needle insertion site
  • Vasovagal reaction during procedure

Rare Complications:

  • Infection at insertion site (<0.1%)
  • Hematoma formation
  • Neuritis or dysesthesia (0.5-1%)
  • Unintended nerve damage

Clinical Evidence

Study Patients Success Rate Follow-up
Atim et al. (2011) 15 73% 6 months
Gopal et al. (2016) 12 83% 12 months
Karaman et al. (2017) 21 76% 9 months
Gunduz et al. (2020) 18 78% 12 months
Case Study Example:

42-year-old female with 3-year history of coccydynia following childbirth. Failed conservative management including NSAIDs, cushions, and physical therapy.

Intervention: Ganglion impar RFA following positive diagnostic block (80% temporary relief)

Outcome: 85% pain reduction at 2 weeks, maintained at 75% at 9-month follow-up. Return to normal activities including prolonged sitting. No complications.

TAME Technique for Coccydynia (Novel Application)

Procedure Steps

  1. Arterial access (radial or femoral artery)
  2. Catheter navigation to pelvic region
  3. Angiography to identify target vessels
  4. Identification of pathological "blush" (hypervascularity)
  5. Selective embolization with Imipenem/Cilastatin
  6. Confirmation of successful occlusion

Target Vessels

  • Median Sacral Artery: Originates from aorta, descends anterior to sacrum and coccyx
  • Lateral Sacral Arteries: Arise from internal iliac artery, supply sacrum and coccyx
  • Focus: Branches showing abnormal hypervascularity ("blush")

Embolic Agent: Imipenem/Cilastatin

  • Forms temporary micro-particles (50-150 µm)
  • Precisely sized to occlude abnormal vessels
  • Spares larger, healthy arteries
  • Temporary occlusion allows for tissue remodeling

Patient Selection for TAME

Ideal Candidates:

  • Chronic coccydynia (pain >3-6 months)
  • Failed conservative treatment (cushions, PT, NSAIDs, local injections)
  • MRI showing edema, contrast enhancement, or hypervascularity around coccyx
  • Desire to avoid surgery

Contraindications:

  • Absolute: Active local infection, severe contrast allergy, uncorrectable coagulopathy
  • Relative: Pregnancy, renal insufficiency, previous adverse reaction to embolic agents
  • Anatomical: Severe vascular anomalies preventing safe catheterization

Advantages of TAME

Minimally Invasive Small puncture site, no large incisions. Reduced pain, minimal scarring, lower infection risk vs. surgery.
Targeted Therapy Addresses underlying pathological process (neoangiogenesis) rather than blocking pain signals.
Preservation of Anatomy Unlike coccygectomy, preserves coccyx and surrounding structures.
Rapid Recovery 1-2 days to resume normal activities vs. weeks/months for surgery.
Reduced Medication Dependence Significant reduction or cessation of long-term pain medications, including opioids.

Recovery & Outcomes

Phase Timeline What to Expect
Early Phase Days 1-7 Possible temporary pain increase (post-embolization syndrome). Mild inflammation response normal. Rest and anti-inflammatory medication as needed.
Intermediate Phase Weeks 1-4 Gradual decrease in pain intensity. Improved mobility and comfort during sitting. Return to normal daily activities.
Peak Improvement Weeks 6-12 Most substantial pain relief achieved. Full effects of reduced inflammation apparent. Tissue remodeling complete.

Need for Recurrent Sessions

  • Single session provides long-lasting relief for many patients
  • May need repeat if: incomplete initial embolization, recurrence of neoangiogenesis, persistent mechanical stress
  • Follow-up angiogram can assess residual or recurrent pathological blush
  • Repeat sessions generally safe and effective

Side Effects and Complications

Common (Mild & Transient):

  • Access site: Minor pain, bruising, hematoma
  • Post-embolization syndrome: Temporary inflammatory response with mild pain, low-grade fever, fatigue (resolves within days)
  • Skin discoloration: Temporary, localized over treated area

Rare but Serious:

  • Non-target embolization: Accidental occlusion of healthy arteries (minimized by precise technique)
  • Vessel injury: Dissection or perforation during catheter manipulation
  • Allergic reaction: To contrast media or medications
  • Infection: Rare at access site
Treatment Comparison
Treatment Mechanism Invasiveness Target
TAME Embolization of abnormal vessels Minimally invasive Inflammation source
RFA Thermal ablation of nerves Minimally invasive Pain signals
Neuromodulation Electrical stimulation Invasive (implant) Pain perception
Coccygeoplasty Bone cement injection Minimally invasive Structural instability
Coccygectomy Surgical removal Highly invasive Pain source removal
Key Advantages of TAME:
  • Disease-modifying vs. symptomatic treatment
  • Preserves anatomical structures
  • Rapid recovery (1-2 days)
  • Outpatient procedure
  • Lower risk profile than surgery
Evidence Base for TAME in MSK Conditions
Knee Osteoarthritis 70% of patients reported >50% pain reduction at 6 months (Okuno et al., 2015)
Frozen Shoulder Mean pain scores decreased from 7.4 to 2.1 at 6 months (Okuno et al., 2018)
Tendinopathies 86% of patients showed clinical improvement at 12 months (Iwamoto et al., 2017)
Plantar Fasciitis Mean VAS pain scores decreased from 7.1 to 1.8 at 6 months (Okuno et al., 2020)

Hypothetical Application to Coccydynia

  • Shared Pathophysiology: Chronic inflammation and neoangiogenesis common to coccydynia and successfully treated conditions
  • Anatomical Feasibility: Well-defined arterial supply (median and lateral sacral arteries) accessible via standard angiographic techniques
  • Imaging Correlation: MRI in chronic coccydynia shows edema/enhancement patterns similar to other inflammatory MSK conditions
  • Translational Potential: Success in other axial skeletal regions suggests potential efficacy for coccyx
Conclusion and Future Directions

Key Conclusions:

  • TAME offers promising, minimally invasive approach for chronic coccydynia by targeting underlying inflammatory pathology
  • Significant advantages over traditional treatments: anatomy preservation, rapid recovery, disease-modifying potential
  • Patient selection critical: chronic pain, failed conservative treatment, imaging evidence of inflammation
  • Strong scientific rationale based on successful TAME applications in other MSK conditions

Future Research Needs

  • Pilot clinical trials specifically for coccydynia
  • Long-term outcome studies (>1 year follow-up)
  • Comparative effectiveness research vs. established treatments
  • Refinement of patient selection criteria

Paradigm Shift: TAME represents a new approach in coccydynia treatment, offering a targeted method that addresses the root cause rather than merely masking symptoms.

Pelvic Congestion Syndrome

Chronic deeply seated unexplained pelvic pain - differential diagnosis with coccydynia. Transcatheter arterial embolization already well-established for treating chronic pelvic pain in women.

10. Shoulder Glenohumeral Instability
Anatomy Guide
AA

Aya Mohamed Abd Elgaleel

Comprehensive MRI Anatomy and Pathology Reference

Shoulder Anatomy & Stability

The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint with very wide range of motion due to smaller socket, making it the most unstable joint in the body.

Most dislocated joint: 85% of shoulder dislocations are glenohumeral.

Stabilizing Structures

Dynamic Restraints:

  • Rotator cuff muscles
  • Long head biceps brachii tendon

Static Restraints:

  • Glenohumeral ligaments (SGHL, MGHL, IGHL)
  • Glenohumeral joint capsule
  • Labrum (glenoid labrum)

Regional Support

Region Structures
Superior Coracoacromial arch, coracoacromial ligament, long head biceps tendon, supraspinatus tendon
Anterior Anterior labrum, glenohumeral ligaments (SGHL, MGHL, IGHL anterior band), subscapularis tendon
Posterior Posterior labrum, posterior band of IGHL, infraspinatus and teres minor tendons
Rotator Cuff

The rotator cuff muscles and tendons stabilize the shoulder joint during movements. Without the rotator cuff, the humeral head would ride up out of the glenoid fossa.

Supraspinatus Tendon

  • Most important rotator cuff structure
  • Subject to tendinopathy and tears
  • Best visualized on coronal oblique and ABER views
  • Axis often rotated anteriorly compared to muscle axis
Large Tears: May allow humeral head to migrate upwards, resulting in high-riding humeral head.
ABER (Abduction-External Rotation) View

Advantages for Assessment

Labral Tears Excellent for anteroinferior labrum (3-6 o'clock position). Stretches inferior GH ligament, creating tension on labrum.
Rotator Cuff Tears Releases tension on cuff, allowing subtle articular-sided partial tears to be visualized with contrast.
Biceps Anchor Better visualization of biceps anchor abnormalities.

Key Structures to Assess

  • Biceps anchor at 12 o'clock
  • Smooth undersurface of supraspinatus tendon
  • Labrum at 3-6 o'clock position
  • Smooth undersurface of infraspinatus tendon
  • Anterior labrum integrity
Labral Variants & Normal Anatomy

Sublabral Recess

Variable attachment of superior labrum at 12 o'clock (biceps insertion):

  • Type I: No recess between glenoid cartilage and labrum
  • Type II: Small recess
  • Type III: Large sublabral recess

Can be difficult to distinguish from SLAP tear or sublabral foramen.

Sublabral Foramen

  • Unattached anterosuperior labrum at 1-3 o'clock position
  • Seen in 11% of individuals
  • Normal variant, not pathologic
  • Differentiation: Sublabral recess is at 12 o'clock only; SLAP tear involves biceps attachment

Os Acromiale

  • Failure of acromial ossification center to fuse
  • Present in 5% of population
  • Usually incidental finding
  • May cause impingement if unstable (pulled inferiorly by deltoid during abduction)
  • Critical: Must mention in report, especially pre-surgery
  • Best seen on superior axial images
Shoulder Dislocations

1. Anterior Dislocation (Most Common)

Antero-inferior dislocation, especially in younger patients results in Bankart fracture/lesion and instability with recurrent dislocations.

Associated Findings:

  • Hill-Sachs Defect (50%): Posterosuperior depression of humeral head
    • Best seen on AP with internal rotation
    • Located at or above coracoid level
    • In first 18mm of proximal humeral head
    • Present in 75-100% with anterior instability
  • Greater tuberosity fracture (15%)

Complications:

  • Recurrent dislocations (40%)
  • Post-traumatic arthritis
  • Injury to axillary nerve or artery
  • Significant bone loss from recurrent dislocations

2. Posterior Dislocation (Rare: 2-4%)

Clinical Challenge: >50% initially unrecognized and misdiagnosed as frozen shoulder

Associated Causes:

  • Epileptic seizures
  • High-energy trauma
  • Electrocution
  • Electroconvulsive therapy

Imaging Signs:

  • Rim Sign (66%): Distance >6mm between medial humeral head border and anterior glenoid rim
  • "Lightbulb Sign": Humeral head fixed in internal rotation
  • Trough Sign (75%): "Reverse Hill-Sachs" - compression fracture of anteromedial humeral head
  • Fracture of posterior glenoid rim
  • Avulsion fracture of lesser tuberosity
Imaging Tip: Isolated lesser tuberosity fractures should raise suspicion of associated posterior dislocation.

3. Superior Dislocation

Very rare type of shoulder dislocation.

Labral Injury Classification

MRI Criteria for Diagnosing Labral Tears

  • Surface irregularity
  • Increased signal within labrum extending to surface
  • Fluid or contrast imbibed into labrum substance
  • Labral avulsions

Secondary Signs:

  • Paralabral cysts
  • Periosteal stripping and tearing
  • Bone injuries (Hill-Sachs, Osseous Bankart)
  • Hyaline cartilage injuries (GLAD lesion)

Common Labral Lesions

Bankart Lesion:

  • Location: Anteroinferior labrum (3-6 o'clock)
  • Complete detachment with tearing of anterior scapular periosteum
  • With/without osseous fragment of glenoid ("Osseous Bankart")
  • Imaging sign: "Double axillary pouch sign"

Reverse Bankart:

  • Location: Posteroinferior labrum (6-9 o'clock)
  • Detachment with tearing of posterior scapular periosteum
  • With/without osseous fragment

SLAP Tears (Superior Labral Anterior-Posterior):

  • Location: Starts at 12 o'clock (biceps anchor)
  • Typically extends from 10 to 2 o'clock position
  • Can extend more posteriorly/anteriorly
  • May extend into biceps tendon
  • Distinguished from Bankart: Bankart is at 3-6 o'clock

Specialized Labral Lesions

Lesion Description Key Features
Perthes Labroligamentous avulsion like Bankart Medially stripped but intact periosteum. Torn labrum may appear normal on neutral positioning. Best seen in ABER position.
ALPSA Anterior Labral Periosteal Sleeve Avulsion Medially displaced labroligamentous complex. Anterior labrum absent from glenoid rim.
GLAD GlenoLabral Articular Disruption Partial tear of anteroinferior labrum with adjacent cartilage damage.
HAGL Humeral Avulsion of inferior Glenohumeral Ligament Discontinuity of IGHL attachment on humerus with contrast leakage.
GAGL Glenoid Avulsion of Glenohumeral Ligament Uncommon. IGHL avulsion from inferior glenoid pole without associated labral disruption.
POLPSA Posterior Labral Periosteal Sleeve Avulsion Posterior equivalent of ALPSA. Associated with posterior instability. Posterior labrum torn, posterior periosteum intact but stripped.
Reverse HAGL Posterior capsule avulsion Complete avulsion of posterior attachment from posterior humeral neck. Tear of posterior IGHL band.
Posterior GLAD Posterior articular cartilage defect Newly described entity. Focal posterior articular defect (7-9 o'clock location).
Bennett Lesion Extra-articular posterior ossification Crescentic posterior ossification associated with posterior labral injury and capsular avulsion. Secondary to posterior subluxation. Best on CT. Often missed by arthroscopy (extra-articular).
Additional Imaging Findings

Muscle Atrophy

Sign of chronic rotator cuff tears. Assess rotator cuff muscle bulk and fatty infiltration.

Paralabral Cysts

Fluid-filled cysts adjacent to labral tears. Secondary sign of underlying labral pathology.

Post-operative Bankart

Follow-up imaging after Bankart repair to assess healing and anchors.

  1. Os acromiale
  2. Supraspinatus tendon alignment with muscle
  3. Biceps tendon attachment at 12 o'clock

  3. Suprascapular nerve and vessels
  4. Supraspinatus impingement by AC joint spurs or thick coracoacromial ligament
  5. Superior biceps-labrum complex
  6. Sublabral recess or SLAP tear
  7. Excessive fluid in subacromial bursa
  8. Supraspinatus tears
  9. Rim-rent tears at anterior fiber insertion
  10. IGHL attachment at humerus
  11. Inferior labral-ligamentary complex
  12. HAGL lesion

  14. Small Hill-Sachs lesions

Sagittal View Checklist

  1. Rotator cuff muscle atrophy
  2. MGHL oblique course and relation to subscapularis
  3. Labral tears at 3-6 o'clock position
  4. Biceps anchor assessment
  5. Acromion shape
  6. AC joint impingement
